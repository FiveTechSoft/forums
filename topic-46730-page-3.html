Hola Carles,

¡Para nada! Rotundamente NO. :)

Mencioné PHP porque allá por el 2008 fue parte de mis primeros experimentos explorando caminos para llevar xBase a la web. Pero NextXbase NO genera PHP ni depende de PHP en lo absoluto.

El flujo y la arquitectura de NextXbase son 100% nativos y modernos:

============================================================

¿CÓMO FUNCIONA REALMENTE NEXTXBASE?

1. CÓDIGO FUENTE xBASE:
Escribes tu aplicación como siempre en tu dialecto preferido: Harbour, FiveWin, Visual FoxPro (VFP), Clipper, Xbase++, etc. Mantienes tus PRG, tu lógica de negocio, tus variables y tus comandos intactos.

2. ARQUITECTURA "MEMORY-SAFE" DE EXTREMO A EXTREMO (END-TO-END):
Aquí está la clave: NO solo el compilador está escrito en Rust; el código que genera y el propio motor de ejecución (runtime) son 100% Rust con seguridad de memoria estricta (Memory-Safe Rust).

  • Cero vulnerabilidades de memoria (sin desbordamientos de búfer, sin corrupción de memoria ni punteros colgados típicos de C/C++).
  • Cumple directamente con los estándares y directivas de seguridad de memoria exigidos por agencias como la NSA y CISA para software moderno.
  • Se compila mediante LLVM a código máquina nativo (x86_64 / ARM) o WebAssembly (WASM).

3. CERO SERVIDORES EXTERNOS NI DEPENDENCIAS:
No hay PHP, no hay Apache, no hay IIS, ni módulos CGI. El ejecutable resultante es completamente autónomo; incorpora su propio servidor HTTP/WebSocket reactivo y asíncrono, capaz de gestionar decenas de miles de conexiones concurrentes en milisegundos con un consumo mínimo de recursos.

4. RENDERIZADO UI DINÁMICO:
En tiempo de ejecución, el motor traduce las ventanas, diálogos y controles del lenguaje a HTML5/CSS/JS moderno y reactivo. Se visualiza con fluidez tanto en cualquier navegador web como en nuestro visor nativo de escritorio.

En resumen: PHP quedó atrás hace muchos años como una simple prueba de concepto. Hoy NextXbase es una solución nativa de alto nivel donde todo —el compilador, el código generado y el motor de ejecución— es Memory-Safe Rust, llevando las aplicaciones del ecosistema xBase (FiveWin, Harbour, Visual FoxPro y Clipper) al estándar de seguridad, robustez y velocidad que la industria exige hoy.

Hello friends,

we should not underestimate the technical foundation we already have with Harbour and FiveWin.

There are three important advantages:

Direct access to the Windows API. We can use Windows functionality without having to wait for new framework components.

C/C++ integration. With #pragma BEGINDUMP and suitable interfaces, we can integrate native code and technologies that are not directly available in Harbour.

Existing business logic. We have applications developed and tested over many years. There is no reason to rewrite everything just because new technologies become available.

And this brings me to an interesting question:

When we talk about the future of Harbour/FiveWin, what time frame do we actually mean? Two years, five years, ten years?

If our existing applications continue to work reliably, we have time to introduce new technologies step by step, even in parallel with our current systems.

Hello Otto,

I agree that Harbour and FiveWin give us a solid technical foundation, and I fully recognize the value of everything we have built over the years.

However, I have a slightly different perspective on the future.

Personally, I believe that in five years, perhaps even sooner, software development will have changed so dramatically that much of what we consider standard technology today may become obsolete.

AI is not simply another technology that we can integrate into our existing applications. It has the potential to fundamentally change how software is designed, developed, maintained and used.

We may move from writing applications ourselves to describing what we need and having intelligent systems build, modify and evolve the software for us.

In that scenario, the question is not simply whether Harbour/FiveWin will survive for another ten years. It is whether our development environment will be able to participate in this new paradigm.

I would certainly preserve our existing applications and take advantage of the enormous amount of proven business logic we already have. But I would be careful not to confuse preserving our investment with preserving our current way of developing software.

Perhaps our biggest challenge is not modernizing Harbour/FiveWin to keep up with today's technologies, but preparing ourselves for a world where the very concept of software development may be completely different.

We should preserve our past, but we must not let it define the limits of our future.
